## Summary for John

- The fracture-gradient formula in M4 is right. Pf = K (S - P) + P is Eaton's equation (E69 p. 1354), Traugott's "standard fracture gradient formula" (T97 pdf p. 2), Zhang and Yin's statement of Matthews & Kelly and Eaton (ZY17 pdf p. 4), IOGP's "effective stress ratio" (IOGP p. 7) and Hauser's Eq. 11 with pore pressure added (Hau20b p. 4). The "not read, to verify" notes on the Eaton form can be closed. Matthews & Kelly 1967 itself is still not read; everything about it comes through Eaton and Zhang & Yin.
- What that curve IS matters more than the number in it. With a constant K it is the minimum horizontal stress, the pressure that re-opens an existing fracture. Leak-off tests sit on that line about 42 % of the time and about 435 psi (3 MPa) higher the rest, up to 1,900 psi (13 MPa) (Hau20b pp. 11, 14). So M4's fracture gradient is a floor on the leak-off pressure, not the leak-off pressure. IOGP says a forecast should show a range from the minimum stress up to breakdown, and calibrate to the low end where data are poor (IOGP pp. 15, 28). M4 shows one line, the low end.
- The number. The engine uses Poisson's ratio 0.40 (K = 0.667) at every depth. On Eaton's Gulf Coast curve that is the value at about 6,000 ft; his curve runs from 0.25 near 1,000 ft to 0.50 at 20,000 ft (E69 p. 1356, read from Fig. 4). From 229 offshore leak-off tests the ratio averages 0.75 (nu 0.43) and FALLS with depth below the mudline (ZY17 pdf pp. 6-7), while Traugott's Gulf of Mexico fit RISES with depth (T97 pdf p. 2). The two published trends disagree in sign (section 6, item 1). My pick (section 10a): keep the constant as the default and add Zhang & Yin's depth curve as a cited option.
- Salt. The engine's "fracture gradient in salt = overburden" now has sources: stress in salt is taken as isotropic and equal to the overburden (Hau20a p. 107), IOGP says the fracture gradient in salt is "generally equal to or above vertical stress" (IOGP p. 15), and Zhang & Yin's 15 Gulf wells put it at overburden plus 0 to 1,000 psi, 500 most likely (ZY17 pdf p. 7). The engine sits at the low end of that band. Below salt the pressure is set by the seal, not by compaction: effective stress at base salt in 24 Gulf wells was 218 to 761 psi (1.5 to 5.25 MPa) (Hau20a p. 109). The engine does not model that and should keep saying so.
- Pore pressure. The engine's compaction law is Hart's equation (H95 p. 45), and the Gulf preset now uses Hart's Gulf shale fit (D27.1). The law cannot give pressure from unloading (gas, clay change, late charge); Hart's own well had about a quarter of its overpressure from such a source (H95 pp. 45, 47), and IOGP says undercompaction methods "severely underpredict" there (IOGP p. 22). The engine's silent clamp of pressure at the overburden (audit F-3) has no equivalent in the data: the highest measured value in Hart's well is 93 % of lithostatic (H95 p. 46).
- A bias worth knowing: the engine's hydrostatic baseline is sea water (1,025 kg/m3, 8.55 ppg). The papers use formation brine for normal pressure: 1.07 g/cc (8.93 ppg) (Hau20a p. 112; HCS p. 7), 10.5 MPa/km (H95 p. 46), 8.5 to 8.95 ppg (T97 pdf pp. 1, 3). The pore-pressure EMW M4 shows is therefore about 0.4 ppg low wherever the excess pressure is small (section 10f).
- Section 10 gives one recommended pick per open item. None is a decision; you decide.

## 1. How overpressure forms, and what the engine models

Mechanisms as the sources describe them:

- **Disequilibrium compaction (undercompaction).** Sediment loaded faster than it can drain keeps its water and its porosity, so pore pressure rises above hydrostatic. IOGP calls it "the common mechanism which generates overpressure in most basin settings" (IOGP p. 7) and the case where basin models work best, "systems dominated by burial-related overpressure (disequilibrium compaction or primary overpressure)" (IOGP p. 18). Hart et al. attribute about three-quarters of the overpressure in their Eugene Island well to it, 15 of 19 MPa (2,200 of 2,750 psi) (H95 pp. 45, 47).
- **Unloading and fluid expansion.** Rocks "cannot 'uncompact'" as effective stress falls, so a late pressure source leaves the porosity of the maximum stress reached (IOGP p. 22, citing Bowers 1995 and Sayers et al. 2001; Bowers to verify; not read). Causes listed: uplift and erosion; a mechanism acting after compaction (diagenesis, gas cracking); pressure transferred along a carrier bed (IOGP p. 22). Hart et al. find the remaining 4 MPa (580 psi) of their overpressure comes from a late source, illitisation, aquathermal pressuring or gas, and that the exponential law then underpredicts because porosity follows a nearly flat unloading curve (H95 p. 47, citing Karig & Hou 1992). IOGP: undercompaction methods "will severely underpredict pore pressure in these geologic settings" (IOGP p. 22).
- **Lateral transfer and the centroid.** Reservoir pressure differs from shale pressure away from the centroid depth; Traugott's rule is about 50 psi per 100 ft of structure above the centroid, and a well at the crest of a high-relief overpressured structure risks losing returns to the seal while flowing from the reservoir (T97 pdf p. 4). IOGP estimates sand pressures separately from shale pressures, by projecting a known pressure along the fluid gradient or by a centroid argument (IOGP p. 21, citing Flemings 2021; to verify; not read).
- **Aquathermal and other secondary mechanisms.** IOGP lists diagenesis, aquathermal expansion, hydrocarbon generation, lateral transfer, uplift and tectonic stress as mechanisms that "typically have no relationship between porosity and effective stress" (IOGP p. 12, citing Swarbrick and Osborne 1998; to verify; not read). Hart et al. name aquathermal pressuring as one candidate for their late source (H95 p. 47). No source in the set quantifies it.
- **Salt seals.** Below an enclosing salt body the pressure is set by the seal: the salt leaks at about 345 psi (2.4 MPa) below the confining stress at the base-salt crest, and pressure below rises at 0.837 psi/ft (0.0189 MPa/m) (Hau20a pp. 110-111). Section 5.

**What the engine models.** `compaction.ts` solves 1D vertical consolidation per column: Athy porosity from the past-maximum Terzaghi effective stress, buoyant load, excess pressure u from a diffusion equation with free drainage at the top and no flow at the base (`compaction.ts` header, lines 3-13). That is disequilibrium compaction only. Unloading exists as a stiffer compressibility (`elastic`, tuned) when the effective stress falls below its past maximum (line 10), which keeps porosity at the maximum-stress value as IOGP describes (IOGP p. 22), but there is no source of late pressure to unload with. Not modelled: lateral transfer (1D, no horizontal flow), the centroid (sand and mud share one cell, so the well reads one pressure; `physics-references.md` M4.1), aquathermal, diagenesis, gas, and salt-sealed pressure (`physics-references.md` M4.1 "Sub-salt pore pressure: not claimed"). The pressure is clamped at the load (`compaction.ts` lines 198-200, `diag.clamps`), which the audit flagged as silent (F-3).

## 2. Effective stress and compaction laws

- **Athy in effective stress.** Hart et al. write phi = phi_0 exp(-beta sigma) with sigma = P_l - P_f, the Terzaghi effective stress, and invert it for pressure, P_f = P_l - (1/beta) ln(phi_0/phi) (H95 p. 45, Eqs. 1-3, citing Rubey & Hubbert 1959 after Athy 1930). Their Gulf Coast shale fit is phi_0 = 0.40 and beta = 3.68 x 10^-2 /MPa, a regression over the hydrostatic section of one well (331 SH #1) to 1,500 m (4,900 ft), sigma to 12 MPa (H95 p. 45, Fig. 2). A second curve, beta = 7.2 x 10^-2 /MPa, matches the deep pressures but overpredicts the shallow ones (H95 p. 47, Fig. 3 caption). The sonic-to-porosity transform behind the fit is Issler's, delta t_ma = 220 microseconds/m, x = 2.19 (H95 p. 46, citing Issler 1992; to verify; not read).
- **Terzaghi is the practice definition.** IOGP: effective stress "is estimated as the difference between the total applied stress and pore pressure" (IOGP p. 6). Hauser's stress model uses sigma_i = S_i - P (Hau20b p. 4, citing Terzaghi 1943). Zhang & Yin note the Biot coefficient is "usually assumed to be 1" in practice (ZY17 pdf p. 4).
- **The Hauser and Couzens-Schultz definition (preprint).** Effective stress as Biot's, sigma = S - alpha P with alpha = 1 - K_fr/K_gr from logs (HCS pp. 4-5, Eqs. 1-2). Their claim: velocity-against-effective-stress trends from 991 sand pressure points in 148 wells are far tighter under Biot than under Terzaghi, and a Terzaghi trend calibrated in a low-pressure basin underpredicts a high-pressure one (HCS pp. 5, 7-8). For soft rock or low pressure the two converge (HCS p. 9). The argument is about log inversion; the authors do not test a forward compaction model, and they state that unloading, complex stress and sand-shale disequilibrium were assumed minimal, not studied (HCS p. 10). Not peer reviewed (HCS, every page).
- **Other compaction forms in the set.** Hauser's sub-salt work uses a two-term power law in void ratio, sigma_v = c1 e^d1 + c2 e^d2, with Terzaghi stress and unprinted constants (Hau20a p. 106). Traugott's default is linear in depth, phi = 0.41 - z/45,455 (z in ft of sediment), "adjusted to fit data" (T97 pdf p. 3). The preprint's trend is bVES = c1 + c2 exp(-c3 e) + c4 exp(-c5 e) with Table 2 constants tied to an unpublished velocity-to-void-ratio trend (HCS p. 7, Table 2).

**What this means for the engine.** `porosity(P, lith, smax)` is phi0 exp(-beta s) with s the past-maximum effective stress (`compaction.ts` lines 30-32): Hart's Eq. 1 exactly, in Pa. The stress is Terzaghi (buoyant load minus excess pressure is total stress minus pore pressure with hydrostatic removed on both sides), so the engine agrees with IOGP p. 6 and Hau20b p. 4; alpha < 1 is not modelled and should be written so in `physics-references.md`. The Gulf preset's mud coefficients after D27.1 are Hart's pair, phi0 0.40 and beta 3.68e-8 /Pa (`presets.ts` line 77); the sand pair stays Sclater & Christie's. Three cautions the papers give: (1) the fit is from the shallow hydrostatic section of one well and its use at 8,000 to 12,000 ft is an extrapolation (H95 p. 45; D27.1 says the same); (2) Hart's phi_0 = 0.40 is a sonic-derived shale value at the top of the logged section, not a seafloor porosity (H95 pp. 45-46), while the engine's phi0 is applied at zero effective stress, i.e. the seafloor, so the preset's shallow mud is denser than Hart's data require (my arithmetic: 0.40 x 1,025 + 0.60 x 2,650 = 2,000 kg/m3, 2.00 g/cc, against Eaton's Gulf Coast surface composite of about 1.91 g/cc, E69 p. 1357 read from Fig. 6, and Traugott's mudline 16.3 ppg = 1.95 g/cc, T97 pdf p. 2); (3) Athy in Terzaghi stress keeps deep sealed mud at phi0 when its effective stress is near zero, which the preprint's data say is not what deep overpressured shale does (HCS p. 4, citing Ebrom et al. 2004; to verify; not read). Page check for the citation card: `presets.ts` line 76 and D27.1 cite Hart's coefficients to p. 46; the deep read places the fit and both constants on p. 45 with Fig. 2 (the second curve is p. 47).

## 3. Pore-pressure prediction from data, and why a forward model is different

- **Eaton ratio methods.** P = O - (O - 8.5)(M/E)^x in ppg, M/E the measured over trend value of resistivity, velocity or travel time; x = 3 for velocity or travel time and 1.2 for resistivity (T97 pdf p. 3, citing Eaton 1976; Eaton 1976 to verify; not read). The sonic exponent "typically 3.0" is repeated by Tahvildari et al. (Tah26 pdf p. 8). The physical basis is that a rise in pore pressure at constant overburden shows as lower velocity or resistivity in shale (IOGP p. 13, citing Eaton 1975 and Bowers 1995; both to verify; not read). Porosity-based methods "are only valid for shales" (IOGP pp. 17, 20).
- **Equivalent depth (vertical) method.** P = O - (O_e - 8.5)(D_e/D), with O_e and D_e the overburden and depth where a vertical line from the measured velocity meets the normal trend (T97 pdf p. 2). Hart's inversion (H95 p. 45, Eq. 3) is the same idea in effective stress.
- **Bowers.** Named by IOGP as a method and as the source of the unloading signature (IOGP pp. 13, 22) and by Hauser & Couzens-Schultz as a trend that differs from theirs because of pressure path (HCS pp. 7-8). To verify; not read.
- **Below salt.** Velocity-based estimates are "much less stable below salt"; in Hauser's 24 wells the sonic response was off trend and scattered while temperature-normalised resistivity was consistent (Hau20a pp. 105, 107, Fig. 4). The preprint's explanation: resistivity is insensitive to effective stress above 10-15 % porosity, so under Terzaghi high-pressure points all plot on its flat part (HCS p. 8, p. 14).
- **How good calibrated prediction gets.** Shale transforms calibrated to sand pressures: 4 % sd, about 10 % maximum (Hau20b p. 8). Classical methods calibrated to MDT points in a carbonate field: RMSE 0.7-1.1 MPa (100-160 psi), a hybrid ML model 0.45 MPa (65 psi) (Tah26 pdf p. 2). IOGP: "a single-point pore pressure prediction for a reservoir is not considered sufficient. It can be misleading and even hazardous" (IOGP p. 20); a range (P10-P50-P90 or discrete scenarios) is required (IOGP pp. 19, 26).
- **Why a forward model is a different thing.** The methods above read pressure from measured logs or seismic through a calibrated trend; the engine computes pressure from a synthetic loading history with no log and no calibration point. IOGP places basin models as the third input, best used "to test the impact of geologic scenarios on the pore pressure profile" (IOGP p. 18), not as the forecast. `physics-references.md` M4 already says the model's excess pressure is "not a real pre-drill prediction (it is the output of a synthetic run)". The one bridge: Hart's Eq. 3 is the engine's law inverted, so any (porosity, overburden) pair the engine reports implies its pore pressure in one line (H95 p. 45), which is a cheap self-consistency test (section 8, row 7).

## 4. Overburden and fracture gradient

**Overburden.** IOGP: vertical stress is "the total vertical load of the static column of formation water and the overlying sediments, calculated from bulk density" (IOGP p. 6), "calculated by integrating the density profile from sea level/ground level to planned well TD" (IOGP p. 19), with density logs preferred and velocity-to-density, a double-exponential fit or literature densities as fallbacks (IOGP pp. 19-20). Eaton's Gulf Coast composite gradient rises from about 0.85 psi/ft at the surface to 1.0 psi/ft at 20,000 ft (E69 p. 1360, Conclusion 4; Fig. 7 p. 1357 reads 0.85 / 0.92 / 0.955 / 1.00 psi/ft at 0 / 6,000 / 10,000 / 20,000 ft, +/- 0.005), from densities of about 1.91 g/cc at the surface to 2.48 g/cc at 20,000 ft (E69 p. 1357, read from Fig. 6, +/- 0.02); he replaced the textbook 1.0 psi/ft with it (E69 p. 1354). Traugott's deep-water form from the derrick floor is O = [8.5 W + rho_avg (D - W - A)]/D with rho_avg = 16.3 + ((D - W - A)/3125)^0.6 ppg; worked values 13.7 ppg at 5,200 ft and 16.7 ppg at 12,300 ft for 2,000 ft of water (T97 pdf pp. 1-2); 1 psi/ft is "not recommended for deepwater" (T97 pdf p. 2). Hauser's recipe is sea water (1.03 g/cc) over the water depth, a fitted a z^b sediment trend, and salt at 2.17 g/cc (0.9408 psi/ft) (Hau20a pp. 107-108, Eq. 1); the uncertainty of a regional trend moves the overburden by less than 2 % (Hau20b p. 8). Engine: `overburdenPa` integrates the record's own bulk density from the sea surface, water column included (`physics-references.md` M4 "Deep water"): the same quantity as all four, with modelled rather than fitted density. The audit found the Gulf preset's overburden 1.5-2 ppg light against Traugott's trend at 8,000-12,000 ft before D27.1 (audit F-4), narrowed to 1.15 ppg at column 150 after it (D27.1).

**Fracture gradient.**

- Eaton keeps Hubbert & Willis's equation, p_w/D = (S/D - p/D) nu/(1 - nu) + p/D, and replaces the constants S/D = 1.0 psi/ft and nu = 0.25 with Gulf Coast depth curves (E69 p. 1354, Eqs. 1-2). His Poisson's ratio is back-calculated from field fracture data with Eq. 4 (E69 p. 1356); on the curve he recommends it reads 0.31 / 0.40 / 0.46 / 0.50 at 2,000 / 6,000 / 10,000 / 20,000 ft, +/- 0.02 (E69 p. 1356, read from Fig. 4), never above 0.50 (E69 p. 1356). Nomograph example: 12,000 ft, p/D 0.67, S/D 0.96, nu 0.46 gives 0.91 psi/ft, 17.5 ppg (E69 p. 1359, Fig. 12). Limits he states: tectonically relaxed areas only (E69 p. 1359); the trend "will not be exactly the same for data from different areas" (E69 p. 1360); breakdown versus extension not separated (E69 p. 1354).
- Matthews & Kelly 1967, via Eaton only: p_w/D = K_i sigma/D + p/D, with K_i read from a Gulf Coast chart at the equivalent depth D_i = sigma/0.535 where the matrix stress would be normal, and an overburden of 1.0 psi/ft (E69 p. 1355, Eq. 3 and procedure). Eaton's two criticisms: the 1.0 psi/ft overburden, and reading K_i at D_i rather than the actual depth (E69 p. 1355). Zhang & Yin restate it as FG = k0 (OBG - Pp) + Pp with k0 from LOT initiation pressures (ZY17 pdf p. 4, Eq. 3). Hauser tests the form on 254 LOTs and finds it the worst of three unless locally calibrated: residual sd 2.66 MPa (385 psi), max 19.1 MPa (Hau20b p. 12, Table 4). The paper itself: to verify; not read (and IOGP's reference list dates it 1965 while its text says 1967, IOGP pp. 25, 41).
- Traugott, deep water: F = k (O - P) + P with k the minimum-to-vertical effective stress ratio; seven estimates of k, of which the engine's are "uniaxial strain" k = nu/(1 - nu) and a constant; his own empirical fit to Gulf of Mexico LOTs is k = 0.039 (D - W/4 - A)^0.33, rising with depth, worked as 0.63 at 5,200 ft and F = 11.6 ppg (T97 pdf p. 2; 0.86 at 12,300 ft is my arithmetic). Every gradient is from the derrick floor, so water depth and air gap lower them all (T97 pdf p. 1).
- Zhang & Yin, 229 offshore LOTs: k0 = (LOT - Pp)/(OBG - Pp) scatters between 0.5 and 1, average 0.75 (ZY17 pdf p. 6, Fig. 4), and decreases with depth below the mudline: k0 = k + a exp(-Z/b), most likely k 0.75, a 0.15, b 7,200 ft (ZY17 pdf p. 7, Eq. 15, Fig. 5), which gives 0.90 at the mudline, 0.79 at 10,000 ft and 0.76 at 20,000 ft below it (my arithmetic). Apparent Poisson's ratios in practice: 0.43 for shale (k 0.75), 0.3 for sandstone (ZY17 pdf p. 4). Bounds: lower = the minimum stress sigma_h = nu/(1-nu)(sigma_V - p) + p; upper = 2 sigma_h - p; most likely the mean of the two (ZY17 pdf p. 6, Eqs. 12-14, citing Zhang 2011). k0 must be calibrated to offset data "because k0 may behave differently for different regions" (ZY17 pdf p. 10).
- IOGP: effective stress ratio (Shmin - P)/(Sv - P), "empirical" (IOGP p. 7); Shmin from a 1D-strain poro-elasto-plastic model or a fault-stability model, calibrated to the fracture closure pressure (IOGP p. 25); fracture pressure "rarely equals the far-field minimum stress" (IOGP p. 26); "In general, the fracture gradient is calibrated to the leak-off pressure (LOP)", and where poorly calibrated "it may be prudent to calibrate the fracture gradient to the lower end of the range" (IOGP p. 15). K0 is extrapolated per stratigraphic unit offshore (IOGP p. 14); horizontal stress is "generally greater in shale than in sandstone and/or limestone" (IOGP p. 25). The plot shows a range from Shmin to breakdown with an "estimated fracture gradient based on average effective stress ratios in between" (IOGP pp. 28, 39).
- Minimum stress versus leak-off. Alberty & McLean define the test points: leak-off (first departure from the modelled linear build-up), breakdown (the peak), propagation, shut-in, and closure, which "can be the most valuable piece of information from the test used to determine the stress state of the formation beyond the influence of the well" (AM14 p. 431); in clastic sections any permeability seen means the test is in sand, which carries a lower minimum stress than shale (AM14 p. 434). Hauser models the LOP as the smaller of re-opening at Shmin and creating a new fracture; the data are bimodal, 42 % at Shmin and 58 % at creation, the gap averaging 3 MPa (435 psi) and reaching nearly 13 MPa (Hau20b pp. 11, 14); Shmin itself is Eq. 11, sigma_h = nu/(1 - nu) sigma_v in Terzaghi stress, valid "strictly" for zero lateral strain and isotropic elasticity (Hau20b p. 4), confirmed by 30 Gulf lost-circulation events none more than 1 MPa from it (Hau20b p. 8). About 10 % of LOTs exceed the overburden (Hau20b p. 11); Zhang & Yin see the same in young Gulf sediments and sub-salt wells (ZY17 pdf p. 8). Hauser also argues Eaton's back-calculated nu trend is an artefact of assuming hydrostatic pressure and treating lost-circulation onset as stress, since "neither stress nor pore pressure were unambiguously available to Eaton (1969)" (Hau20b p. 6).

Engine: `fracturePressurePa(S, P, k) = k (S - P) + P` and `eatonK(nu) = nu/(1 - nu)` (`wellPressure.ts` lines 93-100) are the form above in every source (E69 p. 1354; T97 pdf p. 2; ZY17 pdf p. 4; IOGP p. 7; Hau20b p. 4), in Pa, converted to psi and EMW from RKB (`wellPressure.ts` lines 116-121). With a constant K the curve is the minimum horizontal stress, the re-opening line (Hau20b p. 4), comparable to closure, not to LOT peaks (AM14 p. 431). Section 7 has the formula-by-formula table.

## 5. Salt: pressure and fracture gradient next to, in and below salt

- **Stress in salt.** Isotropic and equal to the overburden, "consistent with common practice in both static and dynamic basin scale numerical models" (Hau20a p. 107, citing Munson and Dawson 1979, Fredrich et al. 2003, Nikolinakou et al. 2018; all three to verify; not read). IOGP: "In salt and anhydrite layers, the fracture gradient is generally equal to or above vertical stress. In front of salt layers, the fracture gradient could also be higher than vertical stress. A tensile strength of 500-800 psi is often considered" (IOGP p. 15, citing Barker and Meeks 2003; to verify; not read). Zhang & Yin, 15 Gulf of Mexico salt wells: most LOT and FIT values lie above sigma_V and below sigma_V + 1,000 psi; P_s = sigma_V + C with C = 500 psi most likely, "an empirical equation only for salt formations", lowered by inclusions and sutures (ZY17 pdf pp. 7-8, Eq. 17, Fig. 6). Salt density: halite 2.168 g/cc, 2.17 used, varying about 0.04 g/cc with temperature and pressure, worth under 70 psi (0.48 MPa) at base salt (Hau20a p. 108).
- **Pore pressure in salt.** "Evaporites are generally impermeable; pore pressure is not relevant because there is not usually any fluid filled pore space" (IOGP p. 21). Inclusions, rafts and interbeds inside salt "may have unexpectedly high pressures", empirically 85-95 % of vertical stress (IOGP p. 21, no source given). Hauser's 13 intra-salt pressure events in 9 wells sit 289-488 psi (1.99-3.36 MPa) below the local overburden, average 413 psi (Hau20a pp. 110-111, Fig. 9). Mud weight against salt creep in sub-salt wells is 80-90 % of the overburden stress (ZY17 pdf p. 7, citing Zhang et al. 2008; to verify; not read).
- **Below salt.** In 24 Gulf wells enclosed by salt, vertical effective stress at base salt is 1.5-5.25 MPa (218-761 psi), mean 3.27 MPa (474 psi), "a rather narrow VES range of less than 4 MPa across a much larger range of overburden loads (75 MPa)" (Hau20a p. 109, Fig. 6). The adopted crestal-leak model: the seal fails about 345 psi (2.38 MPa) below the confining stress at the base-salt overburden minimum, and pressure below the crest rises at 0.837 psi/ft (0.0189 MPa/m), residual sd 90 psi (0.619 MPa) (Hau20a pp. 110-111, Table 1, Eq. 2); that gradient matches the upper bound of 561 regional sand pressures (Hau20a p. 111, Fig. 10). Constant-nu Eaton underestimates the sub-salt fracture gradient in Zhang & Yin's case (ZY17 pdf p. 8, Fig. 7). Caveats: brine only, hydrocarbons may exceed the model; the constants are Gulf allochthon fits and "may vary from region to region" (Hau20a pp. 114-115). Compaction-based transforms assume present effective stress is the historical maximum (Hau20a p. 113).
- **Stress next to salt.** Hauser found no residual correlation with distance below tabular salt in 29 % of one set within 2,500 m and 8 % of another within 2,000 m: "salt tectonics have a negligible impact on stresses for the locations in this study. This assumption should obviously be revisited if more complex salt structures are involved" (Hau20b p. 11). The preprint's Appendix A finds a vertical-stress-only model fits a sub-salt section "independent of distance from the salt body" (HCS pp. 14-15, Fig. A3). IOGP lists "Salt tectonics, diapirism and allochthones rock bodies presence can lead a stress redistribution" among the risks (IOGP p. 23), and 1D overburden "is not exact" unless the surfaces are flat (Hau20a p. 112, citing Fredrich et al. 2003, Nikolinakou et al. 2018; to verify; not read).

Engine: fracture pressure in salt = overburden (`wellPressure.ts` lines 135-136), pore pressure NaN in salt, no window counted in salt (line 142; M4.7). The rule now has three sources and sits at the C = 0, no-tensile-term end of the published band (Hau20a p. 107; IOGP p. 15; ZY17 pdf p. 7). ## 6. Where the literature disagrees

Each item: the claim, each side with pages, status, and which side the engine is on.

1. **Does the stress ratio rise or fall with depth?**
   - Rises: Eaton's Gulf Coast nu goes from about 0.25 at 1,000 ft to 0.50 at 20,000 ft (E69 p. 1356, Fig. 4, surface datum, onshore fracture and lost-circulation data). Traugott's k = 0.039 (D - W/4 - A)^0.33 rises below the mudline (T97 pdf p. 2, Gulf of Mexico LOTs, no data shown); he also writes "because k tends to increase with depth" (T97 pdf p. 4). Via Eaton, the Matthews & Kelly K_i curves rise from 0.45 to 1.0 over 2,000-20,000 ft (E69 p. 1355, read from Fig. 3).
   - Falls: Zhang & Yin's 229 offshore LOTs give k0 decreasing with depth below the mudline, 0.90 at the mudline to 0.76 at 20,000 ft (ZY17 pdf p. 7, Eq. 15, Fig. 5, my arithmetic for the values).
   - Neither is a stress trend: Hauser holds that Eaton's trend is an artefact of his assumptions (Hau20b p. 6) and that Shmin follows nu/(1-nu) with the present nu whatever the loading history (Hau20b p. 5); IOGP treats the ratio as empirical and per unit (IOGP pp. 7, 14).
   - Status: open. Part of it is datum (surface versus mudline) and part is that LOT peaks mix re-opening and creation (Hau20b p. 11), so a ratio from LOT peaks is not a stress ratio. **Engine:** constant K; neither side. Section 10a.
2. **What the "fracture gradient" curve is: minimum stress or leak-off.**
   - Minimum stress / closure: AM14 p. 431 (closure is the stress state); IOGP p. 25 (Shmin calibrated to closure) and p. 26 (fracture pressure rarely equals Shmin); Hau20b p. 4 (Eq. 11 is Shmin, the re-opening pressure).
   - LOT equals fracture gradient: Zhang & Yin assume LOT = FG while noting the LOT "may not be the breakdown pressure" (ZY17 pdf p. 6); Traugott's k and Eaton's nu are fitted to leak-off and fracture data (T97 pdf p. 2; E69 p. 1356); IOGP's operational definition is the pressure at which losses are noted (IOGP p. 7) and the FG is "in general" calibrated to the LOP (IOGP p. 15).
   - Status: terms, but with a numerical consequence of about 435 psi average, up to 1,900 psi (Hau20b p. 11). **Engine:** the minimum-stress side (constant K). It should be labelled so.
3. **Terzaghi or Biot effective stress.**
   - Terzaghi: IOGP p. 6; Hau20b p. 4; H95 p. 45; ZY17 pdf p. 4 (alpha "usually assumed to be 1").
   - Biot with log-derived alpha: HCS pp. 4-5, 7-8 (preprint, not peer reviewed).
   - Status: open for log inversion; not tested for forward compaction (HCS p. 10). **Engine:** Terzaghi, with the practice documents; Biot "not modelled".
4. **Fracture gradient in salt: overburden, or overburden plus a term.**
   - Equal to overburden: Hau20a p. 107 (isotropic stress in salt, common modelling practice).
   - Above it: ZY17 pdf p. 7 (sigma_V + 500 psi most likely, 0-1,000 psi); IOGP p. 15 ("equal to or above", 500-800 psi tensile term often added).
   - Status: consistent once the terms are separated (stress = Sv; test pressure = Sv plus a tensile or near-wellbore term). **Engine:** Sv, the low end. Section 10b.
5. **A constant ratio or a locally calibrated one.**
   - A usable constant: Zhang & Yin suggest k0 = 0.75 as "most likely" for Matthews & Kelly (ZY17 pdf p. 6); apparent nu 0.43 for shale (ZY17 pdf p. 4).
   - Calibrate locally or do not use: Hauser, the empirical approach "performs poorly on the full data set ... it must simply be calibrated on a local scale" (Hau20b p. 12); Eaton, repeat the back-calculation per area (E69 p. 1359); IOGP, per stratigraphic unit (IOGP p. 14).
   - Status: settled that a constant is a local empirical number. **Engine:** one tuned constant per well (0.40, K 0.667; Ki 0.60), labelled tuned, which is the honest label.
6. **Does salt perturb the stress in the sediment beside and below it?**
   - Negligible for tabular salt: Hau20b p. 11; HCS pp. 14-15.
   - Significant, or needs 3D: IOGP p. 23 (stress redistribution a listed risk); Hau20a p. 112 (1D overburden not exact under complex geometry); Hau20b p. 11 itself ("revisited if more complex salt structures are involved").
   - Status: open, geometry dependent. **Engine:** no perturbation; the fracture gradient beside a salt flank is computed as elsewhere. `physics-references.md` M4 already lists "halite near a salt flank or a weld" under not claimed.
7. **Sub-salt pore pressure: a compaction result or a seal limit.**
   - Seal limit: Hau20a pp. 109-111 (near-constant base-salt VES, crestal-leak model).
   - Compaction with salt as a lid: no source in the set argues it; IOGP says nothing quantitative about sub-salt pressure (IOGP p. 21 covers evaporites only).
   - Status: one published side. **Engine:** neither; "not claimed". Section 10f.
8. **Mean or vertical effective stress in equivalent-depth methods.** Traugott recommends the mean stress (O - P)(1 + 2k)/3 and ends the suggestion with a question mark (T97 pdf p. 4); every other source uses vertical effective stress (H95 p. 45; IOGP p. 13; Hau20b p. 4). Status: a lone suggestion. **Engine:** vertical, with the majority.
9. **Matthews & Kelly's K_i: at the equivalent depth or the actual depth.** M&K read it at D_i (E69 p. 1355, via Eaton); Eaton recommends the actual depth (E69 p. 1355); Zhang & Yin's restatement has no D_i step (ZY17 pdf p. 4). **Engine:** a constant at the actual depth, i.e. Eaton's reading, not M&K's procedure. The docs should say so.
10. **Which log works below salt.** Resistivity consistent, sonic scattered (Hau20a p. 107); the preprint explains it as a Terzaghi artefact that disappears under Biot (HCS p. 14). Not an engine matter; recorded because it bears on any future comparison of the model with sub-salt data.

## 10. Inputs for the open M4 and audit items (options only; John decides)

Each item: the options, ONE recommended pick, and the reason. Nothing here is a decision.

**(a) Stress ratio rising with depth below the mudline (Backlog, M4.6 Finding 3).**
- A. Keep one constant K, relabel it as a mid-depth value (E69 p. 1356: 0.40 is the recommended curve at about 6,000 ft).
- B. Traugott's k = 0.039 (D - W/4 - A)^0.33, rising (T97 pdf p. 2). Constants are the author's Gulf of Mexico LOT fit with no data shown; a LOT-peak fit, so above Shmin.
- C. Zhang & Yin's k0 = 0.75 + 0.15 exp(-Z/7,200 ft), falling below the mudline (ZY17 pdf p. 7, Eq. 15), from 229 offshore LOTs with low and high sets given (0.5, 0.1, 5,100; 0.9, 0.4, 12,500). Also a LOT-peak fit.
- D. Eaton's nu(depth) from Fig. 4 as a table (E69 p. 1356, read +/- 0.02, onshore surface datum); Hauser argues the trend is an artefact (Hau20b p. 6).
- **Recommended: A as the default plus C as a third selectable method, labelled "leak-off estimate (Zhang & Yin 2017)".** Reason: the two published trends disagree in sign (section 6, item 1), so no single depth curve can be called "the" answer; C is the only one with a stated dataset (229 tests), a mudline datum that matches the engine's deep-water setting, and printed constants with a range; B's constants cannot be checked and D is disputed. Keeping the constant-K curve as Shmin and adding C as the leak-off estimate gives the engine the two edges IOGP's plot expects (IOGP p. 28) without pretending either is calibrated. Cost: one function and a registry option; no engine state. The default stays as it is, so every earlier run is unchanged.

**(b) Fracture gradient in salt (the engine sets it to the overburden, no source).**
- A. Keep Pf = Sv and cite it (Hau20a p. 107; IOGP p. 15; ZY17 pdf p. 7 with C = 0).
- B. Sv + 500 psi, Zhang & Yin's most likely C from 15 Gulf wells (ZY17 pdf p. 7, Eq. 17), with the 0-1,000 psi band stated.
- C. Sv plus a 500-800 psi tensile term (IOGP p. 15, citing Barker and Meeks 2003; to verify; not read).
- **Recommended: A.** Reason: Sv is what the stress IS in salt (Hau20a p. 107); B and C are test pressures that include a tensile or near-wellbore term, inconsistent with the engine's sediment curve being Shmin (item (a)); and the window is not counted in salt (M4.7), so B or C would move a plotted line and nothing else. Close the "(to verify)" with the three citations and state the band.

**(c) A leak-off or minimum-stress limit on pore pressure below the overburden (audit F-3).**
- A. Status quo: silent clamp of u at the load.
- B. Surface the clamp: show `diag.clamps` in the physics checks, mark clamped cells in the pseudo-well and the well CSV, and document the limit (the audit's own recommendation; H95 p. 46 gives 93 % of lithostatic as the published maximum).
- C. A vertical-effective-stress floor as a cited Gulf rule: Hauser's seal strength about 345 psi (2.38 MPa, sd about 169 psi) below the confining stress at the base-salt crest, VES 218-761 psi across 24 wells (Hau20a pp. 109-110); fluid leaving the cell when the floor binds would have to be removed from the mass ledger.
- D. A hydrofracture cap at Shmin: not available, since P <= k(S - P) + P holds for every P <= S, so a Shmin cap is the same as the present clamp (my arithmetic).
- **Recommended: B now, C as a separate later card, GoM-only and salt-sealed-only.** Reason: B changes no physics and makes the F-3 finding visible, which is what the audit asked; C is the only cited number in the set, but it describes salt-sealed sediment, the regional set is only the upper-bound gradient of 561 sand pressures (Hau20a p. 111), and a floor that vents fluid touches the per-material mass balance and needs its own benchmark. Never tune the floor to a result.

**(d) A depth floor for the narrowest-window flag (M4.8 N4).**
- A. No floor; the flag names the conductor interval on land and the mudline offshore.
- B. A per-well "surface casing shoe" depth below ground or mudline, default 0 (off); the narrowest window, tight and no-window runs are reported below it, with the whole-well result still listed. - C. A computed floor where the EMW sensitivity of a fixed pressure error falls below the tight threshold (Hau20b p. 6: 0.5 MPa is 1.4 ppg at 300 m below mudline and 0.14 ppg at 3,000 m).
- **Recommended: B.** Reason: it is what a Gulf engineer asked for in N4 and what IOGP's plot carries (casing points, IOGP p. 19); the default off keeps every existing CSV identical; C depends on an arbitrary pressure error. If a non-zero default is wanted, 3,000 ft is the one published example (E69 p. 1358) and would be labelled tuned.

**(e) Gulf overburden and compaction after D27.1 (Hart's second curve, densities, the Traugott gap).**

Add a physics check (not a tuning) that compares a pseudo-well overburden with Traugott's deep-water trend, O = [8.5 W + (16.3 + ((D - W - A)/3125)^0.6)(D - W - A)]/D ppg (T97 pdf pp. 1-2), and with Eaton's onshore composite (E69 p. 1357) for a land wellhead, reporting the gap in ppg: the audit F-4 recommendation.
